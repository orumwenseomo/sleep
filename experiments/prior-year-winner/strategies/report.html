Follow-up · S&P 500 · 2006 to Aug 2026

Which rule wins more often?

The reel's rule, buying last year's single best S&P 500 stock, beat the index in about half of its twenty years. This page tries 41 mechanical rules built from the same raw material, prior returns and volatility of index members, and scores each one by how many years it beat the S&P 500 total-return index, not just by how much money it made.

Nothing wins reliably, but a small basket of last year's winners is the most consistent thing in the set.

The best hit rate is 14 of 20 years, shared by two near-identical rules: the top three stocks of the prior year, held equal-weight for one year, and the top three by twelve-month momentum skipping December. Both compounded at 17 to 21 percent a year against 11 percent for the index. A random three-stock basket reaches 14 wins about 3 percent of the time, so across 41 rules a result like this was more likely than not to appear by luck. The pattern that survives scrutiny is structural: spreading the bet from one stock to three to ten raises the hit rate from 10 to 14 to 12 and lifts the median year from a loss to a gain. Nearly all of that edge came after 2014.

When each rule won

A hit rate hides when the wins came. This grid shows each year's excess return over the index for the most instructive rules. Blue is a year ahead of the index, red a year behind, and the shade is the size of the gap. Read across a row and the regime dependence is obvious: baskets of prior-year winners lost most of 2006 to 2014 and then won almost everything from 2015 on, while the defensive rules did the reverse.

What the numbers say

  • Diversifying the winners helps consistency more than anything else. From the #1 stock to the top 3 to the top 10: hit rate 10, 14, 12; median year -1.3, +12.5, +3.7 points; hold-to-2026 wins 12, 15, 16 of 20. The single stock has the fattest tails (Nvidia 2017, AMD 2019, Nvidia 2024) and the worst medians.
  • Single-rank picks are noise. The #1, #2, #3, #5 and #10 stocks of the prior year won 10, 7, 13, 6 and 7 years. Number three looks brilliant only because it landed on Nvidia in 2016, a 215-point year.
  • The edge is a 2015-to-2025 story. The top-3 basket beat the index in 4 of 9 years to 2014 and 10 of 11 after. Its bad years were brutal: -56% in 2008, 36 points behind in 2009, 35 behind in 2011, 20 behind in 2023.
  • Defensive filters top out at 11 wins and give up the upside. Lowest-volatility baskets and "beat the index last year but low-vol" baskets win in 2008, 2011 and 2022 and lose in the up years; held to 2026 they beat their index twin in 1 to 5 years out of 20.
  • Contrarian rules are coin flips with fat tails. Buying the worst stock of the prior year won 9 years and compounded at -24% a year. This family is also the one survivorship bias flatters most, because the names that went to zero are not in the data.
  • The base rate is falling. Around half of index members beat the cap-weighted index in a typical year to 2022, but only 28 to 31 percent in 2023 to 2025, when a few mega-caps carried the index. Every equal-weight rule now starts the year behind.

So what would you actually do?

If the goal is to beat the index in more years than not, the only rules here that got to 70 percent are small baskets of last year's biggest winners, rebalanced annually. Treat that as a hypothesis, not a finding: it is the momentum effect the academic literature has documented since the 1990s, it worked spectacularly in the 2015 to 2025 growth regime, and it did nothing in the nine years before that. It also carries the full cost of being wrong, with a -56% year in the sample, plus 100 percent annual turnover and the taxes that go with it. Nothing tested here beats the index reliably, and a 14-of-20 record from 41 attempts is exactly what luck would produce. A plain index fund won every one of these comparisons on consistency per unit of risk.

Method and caveats

  • Universe: members of the S&P 500 at the prior year-end (constituent history from the fja05680/sp500 dataset), limited to the 700 tickers Yahoo Finance still serves. About 270 delisted names are missing, which flatters every rule and the contrarian ones most.
  • Prices: Yahoo monthly split- and dividend-adjusted closes; the ratio of two closes is a total return. Selection uses only data through the prior December. Baskets are equal-weight. No transaction costs or taxes.
  • Null distribution: 800 random equal-weight baskets per size, drawn from the same universe each year. Mean wins 9.1 (1 stock), 9.5 (3 stocks), 9.7 (10 stocks); 14 or more wins occurs 1.6%, 3.4% and 2.9% of the time.
  • Code and data are in the repository alongside this page (experiments/prior-year-winner/strategies).
